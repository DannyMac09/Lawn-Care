## Chapter 1: The Rod, Reel & Line Checklist

Walk into a tackle shop and you'll see a wall of rods that all look the same. Here's what actually matters for a regular fishing trip.

### The One Combo That Covers Almost Everything

If you own one setup, make it a **medium-power, 6'6" to 7' spinning rod** with a **2500-size spinning reel**. This handles bass, panfish, catfish, trout, and walleye without breaking a sweat. A medium spinning rod and reel combo is the best money a beginner can spend — one rod, every common freshwater fish.

### Rod Vocabulary (60 Seconds)

* **Power** = how much muscle the rod has. Ultra-light (tiny panfish), light, **medium-light**, **medium** (the all-rounder), medium-heavy, heavy (big catfish, pike).
* **Action** = where the rod bends. Fast action bends near the tip (sensitive, good hooksets). Slow action bends through the whole rod (forgiving, good for beginners and kids). **Medium or medium-fast action** is the sweet spot for most people.
* **Length** = longer casts farther. 6'6" to 7' is the everyday standard. Shorter (5'6"–6') for kids or tight creeks with overhanging trees.

### Reels in Plain English

* **Spinning reel:** The open-face reel that hangs under the rod. Easiest to learn, fewest tangles, the right choice for almost everyone. If you're new, start here.
* **Baitcasting reel:** Sits on top of the rod, casts farther and more accurately — but punishes beginners with bird's nests (tangled line). Learn on a spinning reel first.
* **Spincast (push-button) reel:** The closed-face reel with the button on the back. Dead simple — great for young kids. Less casting distance and drag control than spinning.

### Line — Keep It Simple

* **Monofilament (mono):** The standard clear or green line. Cheap, stretchy (forgiving on hooksets), easy to knot. The right line for 90% of trips. **8-lb test** is the universal freshwater choice; drop to **6-lb** for panfish and trout, go up to **12-lb** for catfish.
* **Braided line:** Thin, strong, no stretch — great sensitivity. But it's visible in clear water and harder to knot well. Tie a 3-foot mono or fluorocarbon leader to the end with a double uni knot if you use it.
* **Fluorocarbon:** Nearly invisible underwater, sinks faster. Good as a leader for spooky fish in clear water. Stiffer and pricier than mono — overkill as a full spool for most people.

**Pre-trip line check:** Run the last 2 feet of line through your fingers. If it feels nicked, rough, or curly, cut it off and retie. Old line is the #1 cause of lost fish — a season-old spool of mono that's been baking in the garage has lost real strength. When in doubt, respool: 8-lb monofilament line.

### The Checklist

- [ ] Rod — right power/length for the target (medium 6'6"–7' covers most)
- [ ] Reel — spinning reel, drag working smoothly, handle tight
- [ ] Line — fresh enough, correct test (8-lb mono default), no nicks in the last 2 feet
- [ ] Spare spool or extra line in the tackle bag
- [ ] Rod case or socks for transport (guides are fragile and expensive)
- [ ] Pliers or multitool (hook removal, crimping)
- [ ] Line clippers or nail clippers
- [ ] Tape measure or ruler (for length limits)
- [ ] Landing net (a rubber-mesh landing net is gentler on fish than knotted nylon)
- [ ] Stringer or livewell/basket if keeping fish

## Chapter 2: Tackle Box Essentials by Target

You don't need a wall of lures. For each of the three most common freshwater targets, here's the short list that actually catches fish. Pack the section for what you're after, plus the shared basics.

### Shared Basics (every trip)

- [ ] Assorted hooks — #6 and #4 baitholder hooks (the ones with little barbs on the shank) for live bait, plus a few #2 for bigger baits
- [ ] Split shot sinkers — assorted small (pinch onto the line with pliers)
- [ ] Egg or bullet sinkers — 1/4 oz and 1/2 oz
- [ ] Bobbers — a few round red-and-white ones (1" for panfish, 1.5" for bigger bait)
- [ ] Swivels — size 10 barrel swivels (stop line twist)
- [ ] Bobber stops (if you ever fish deeper than your rod is long)
- [ ] Live bait container + aerator if bringing minnows
- [ ] Scent/attractant (optional — a garlic or shad scent spray helps on slow days)

### Bass

Bass are aggressive and eat anything that looks alive. You need a small selection that covers the water column — top, middle, bottom.

- [ ] Plastic worms — 6" and 7" in green pumpkin and black (Texas-rigged on a 3/0 worm hook with a 1/4 oz bullet weight — the single most reliable bass setup ever made)
- [ ] Jig heads — 1/8 oz and 1/4 oz (pair with curly-tail grubs in white or chartreuse)
- [ ] Spinnerbait — 3/8 oz in white or chartreuse (covers water fast to find active fish)
- [ ] Topwater popper or frog (dawn and dusk — the most fun bite in fishing)
- [ ] Crankbait — one shallow diver (dives 2–5 feet) in a shad or crawfish color
- [ ] Live bait backup: shiners or nightcrawlers on a #2 hook under a bobber

### Panfish (Bluegill, Crappie, Perch)

Panfish are plentiful, bite readily, and are perfect for kids — but they have small mouths, so go small.

- [ ] #6 or #8 long-shank hooks (easier to unhook from small mouths)
- [ ] 1" bobbers — set 2 to 4 feet deep to start
- [ ] 1/32 oz and 1/16 oz jig heads
- [ ] 1"–2" soft plastic grubs or tubes in white, chartreuse, or pink
- [ ] Live bait: red worms, wax worms, or small minnows — the #1 panfish bait, period
- [ ] Small split shot — just enough to get the bait down

### Catfish

Catfish hunt by smell, mostly at night and in murky water. Big hooks, stink bait, patience.

- [ ] Circle hooks — 5/0 to 7/0 (they hook the fish in the corner of the mouth by themselves — just let the rod load up, don't jerk)
- [ ] 1/2 oz to 1 oz egg or no-roll sinkers (enough weight to hold bottom in current)
- [ ] Barrel swivels and a 12"–18" leader
- [ ] Bait: nightcrawlers (always work), chicken liver (classic — wrap it in a bit of pantyhose or mesh so it stays on the hook), cut shad or bluegill, stink bait/dough bait
- [ ] Rod holder or forked stick — catfishing is a waiting game, and you want the rod secured

### How to Pack the Box

Keep it simple: one tray per target, hooks and terminal tackle in a separate small box so you're not digging. Every trip, spend 5 minutes before you leave doing the **tackle audit**: check that hooks are sharp (drag the point across your thumbnail — if it digs in, it's sharp; if it slides, replace it), replace rusted hooks, and restock whatever you lost last time. A compartment tackle box with adjustable dividers keeps it all organized.

## Chapter 3: Licenses & Regs — Don't Skip This

This chapter is short because the rule is simple: **check your state, keep your license on you, know the limits.** Game wardens don't accept "I didn't know."

### The License

* **Almost every state requires a fishing license for anyone 16 or older** (some states it's 15 or 18 — check yours). Kids usually fish free under that age, but the adult supervising them needs a license.
* **Buy it before the trip.** Most states sell licenses online — you can usually print a temporary one or keep the digital copy on your phone. Some states also sell them at bait shops, sporting goods stores, and big-box stores.
* **Keep it on you.** On your phone or in your wallet — but on your person, not in the truck. If a warden checks you streamside and the license is in the truck a mile away, that's a ticket.
* **Know what type you need.** Freshwater vs. saltwater, resident vs. non-resident, and special stamps (trout stamps are common — if you're fishing for trout and your state requires one, get it).
* **Licenses expire.** Most run on a calendar year or 365 days from purchase. Check the date before every season's first trip.

### The Regulations

* **Size and bag limits vary by water, not just by state.** The lake you're on may have a 15-inch minimum on bass while the river 10 miles away doesn't. Check the specific regulations for the specific water you're fishing — your state's wildlife agency website or app has them.
* **Catch-and-release rules** apply in some areas and seasons. If the fish doesn't meet the minimum size, release it quickly and gently (wet your hands first — dry hands strip the fish's protective slime).
* **Bait restrictions exist.** Some waters ban live minnows (to prevent spreading invasive species) or restrict the number of rods you can fish with at once. Know before you bait up.
* **Private ponds still have rules.** If it's not your pond, you need the landowner's permission AND usually still need your state license.

**The 5-minute pre-trip check:** state license valid? Correct stamps? Size/bag limits for this water? Bait legal here? Done — you're legal.

## Chapter 4: The Safety Checklist

Fishing is safe until it isn't. Water, hooks, sun, and weather are the four things that hurt people — all of them are manageable with a checklist.

### On the Water

- [ ] **Life jackets (PFDs)** — one per person, worn (not just aboard) by kids and weak swimmers. In a boat or kayak, everyone wears one, no exceptions. A comfortable fishing life vest is the difference between wearing it and "forgetting" it.
- [ ] **Tell someone your plan.** Where you're going, when you expect to be back. If you're going alone, this is non-negotiable — text a person, not just a vague "going fishing."
- [ ] **Check the weather before AND during.** Storms build fast on the water. If you hear thunder, you're already too late getting off — head in at the first rumble. Lightning + open water + graphite rods is a combination you never want to test.
- [ ] **Wading safety:** Wear a wading belt, shuffle your feet (don't step blindly), use a wading staff in current, and never wade deeper than mid-thigh in moving water. Felt soles or cleats on slippery rock.
- [ ] **Boat basics:** Kill switch attached to you, navigation lights if you're out near dawn/dusk, and know where the nearest ramp is.

### Hooks, Sun, and Bugs

- [ ] **First aid kit** — at minimum: bandages, antiseptic wipes, tweezers (for hook barbs and ticks), Benadryl, pain reliever, and any personal meds. A compact waterproof first aid kit lives in the tackle bag permanently.
- [ ] **Hook removal know-how:** Push the hook through, snip the barb with pliers, back it out. For a deeply embedded hook or anything near an eye or joint — leave it and get medical help. Pliers and side-cutters in the bag, always.
- [ ] **Sunscreen and a hat.** Water reflects sun from below — you burn faster fishing than hiking. Reapply every 2 hours. Polarized sunglasses aren't just for comfort: they cut surface glare so you can actually see fish and structure.
- [ ] **Bug spray.** Ticks near the bank, mosquitoes at dusk. Check for ticks after the trip — all of you, including the dog.
- [ ] **Water and snacks.** Dehydration sneaks up on you standing in the sun. A gallon of water per person for a full day, minimum.
- [ ] **Knife or multitool** — cutting line, cutting bait, a hundred small jobs.

### The Night-Fishing Add-On

- [ ] Headlamp (plus a spare) — and a red-light mode so you don't blind everyone every time you tie a knot
- [ ] Extra batteries
- [ ] Glow sticks or clip-on lights for rod tips (so you can see a bite in the dark)
- [ ] Tell someone, again — night trips get the float plan double-underlined

## Chapter 5: Species Quick-Reference

The common freshwater fish you're most likely to run into, where they live, and what to offer them. Generic, works-anywhere advice — your state's wildlife site has the local details.

| Species | Where to Find Them | What They Bite |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Largemouth Bass** | Weeds, lily pads, fallen trees, docks — anywhere with cover in warm, calm water. Ambush predators; they sit in cover and rush out. | Plastic worms (Texas rig), jigs, spinnerbaits, topwater at dawn/dusk, live shiners |
| **Smallmouth Bass** | Clear, cool rivers and rocky lakes. Around boulders, ledges, current breaks. Fight harder per pound than almost anything. | Tube jigs, small crankbaits, Ned rigs, live crayfish or hellgrammites |
| **Bluegill / Sunfish** | Shallow weed edges, docks, anywhere near shore in warm months. Travel in schools — where there's one, there are twenty. | Red worms or wax worms on a #8 hook under a bobber, 2–4 feet deep. The easiest fish to catch, period. |
| **Crappie** | Brush piles, submerged timber, docks — suspended in the water column, often 6–12 feet down. School tightly. | 1/16 oz jigs with 1"–2" soft plastics (white/chartreuse), live minnows under a bobber |
| **Channel Catfish** | Deep holes, river bends, below dams — anywhere the current slows and food settles. Most active at night and after rain. | Nightcrawlers, chicken liver, cut bait, stink bait — on the bottom, with patience |
| **Rainbow / Brook / Brown Trout** | Cold, clear, oxygen-rich streams and lakes. Need water under ~65°F. Spooky — they see you before you see them. | Small inline spinners, salmon eggs, worms drifted naturally with the current, small spoons. Light line (4–6 lb), quiet approach. |
| **Walleye** | Deep, cool lakes and rivers — rocky points, drop-offs, weed edges. Low-light feeders: dawn, dusk, overcast days. | Jigs tipped with minnows or leeches, live-bait rigs, crankbaits trolled along structure |
| **Yellow Perch** | Weed beds and sandy flats in lakes. School in big numbers; great eating. | Small minnows, worms, 1/16 oz jigs — fished near bottom |

**The universal starter rig:** If you don't know what's in the water, tie on a #6 baitholder hook, pinch on a split shot 12 inches above it, thread on half a nightcrawler, and hang it 3 feet under a bobber. Cast near cover. This rig has caught every species on this list.

## Chapter 6: The Three Knots You Actually Need

Hundreds of fishing knots exist. You need three. Each one below is described step by step — read it with line in your hands and you'll have it in five minutes.

### 1. The Improved Clinch Knot — your everyday knot

**When to use it:** Tying line to a hook, lure, or swivel. This is your default knot for almost everything.

**The steps:**
1. Thread the end of the line through the eye of the hook. Pull about 6 inches through.
2. Wrap the loose end around the standing line (the part going back to your rod) **5 to 7 times**, working away from the hook. Keep the wraps neat and snug against each other.
3. Take the loose end and thread it through the small loop that formed right at the hook eye (between the eye and the first wrap).
4. Now thread the loose end through the bigger loop you just created — the one between the wraps and the hook.
5. Wet the knot with saliva (this is important — it lets the wraps slide tight without burning the line).
6. Pull the standing line steadily while holding the hook. The wraps will slide down and cinch tight against the eye.
7. Trim the loose end to about 1/8 inch.

**Test it:** Give it a hard pull. If it slips, you didn't wet it or didn't pull steadily — cut and retie. Takes 30 seconds once you've done it ten times.

### 2. The Palomar Knot — the strongest simple knot

**When to use it:** Tying on hooks and lures, especially with braided line (braid slips in a clinch knot — use Palomar instead). Roughly the strongest knot you can tie, and it's nearly foolproof.

**The steps:**
1. Double about 6 inches of line back on itself to form a loop.
2. Thread the LOOP (not the end) through the eye of the hook. Pull the loop through so the hook hangs on the doubled line.
3. Tie a simple overhand knot in the doubled line — just like the first step of tying your shoes — but don't tighten it. Leave it loose and open.
4. Pass the hook itself up through the big loose loop of the overhand knot.
5. Wet everything, then pull both the standing line and the loose end steadily. The knot slides down and locks against the eye.
6. Trim the loose end.

**Why it's great:** You can tie it in the dark, with cold hands, in about 20 seconds. When in doubt, Palomar.

### 3. The Loop Knot (Non-Slip Loop) — for lures that need to move

**When to use it:** Tying on jigs, crankbaits, and topwater lures — anything that needs to swing and wiggle freely. A knot pulled tight against the eye restricts a lure's action; a loop knot lets it dance.

**The steps:**
1. Tie a loose overhand knot in the line about 4 inches from the end. Don't tighten it — leave it open.
2. Thread the end of the line through the lure's eye, then back up through the loose overhand knot.
3. Wrap the end around the standing line **3 to 5 times**.
4. Thread the end back down through the overhand knot (the same one from step 1), going the opposite direction from step 2.
5. Wet it, hold the lure, and pull the standing line steadily to cinch. The overhand knot locks the wraps, leaving a small loop at the lure eye.
6. Trim the end.

**The check:** The lure should swing freely on the loop. If the loop closed tight against the eye, you pulled the wrong end — cut and redo it.

## Chapter 7: When the Fish Aren't Biting

Every angler gets skunked. The difference between a bad day and a learning day is working through this list instead of just casting the same thing to the same spot for six hours.

### 1. Change Depth

Fish move up and down in the water column with light, temperature, and food. If you've fished the surface and the bottom with nothing, fish the middle — and vice versa. Under a bobber, slide it up or down a foot at a time. With a jig, count it down longer before you start retrieving. **The most common mistake in fishing is fishing 3 feet above or below where the fish actually are.**

### 2. Change Bait Size and Color

* **Go smaller first.** When fish are finicky, a smaller offering gets bit when a big one gets ignored. Drop from a 7" worm to a 4", or from a 1/4 oz jig to a 1/16 oz.
* **Then change color.** The rule of thumb: **bright colors in murky water** (chartreuse, white, orange — fish need to see it), **natural colors in clear water** (green pumpkin, brown, shad patterns — match what they're eating). If you've thrown natural for an hour in clear water with nothing, try something obnoxious — sometimes a reaction bite is all it takes.
* **Switch to live bait.** When artificials fail, a real worm or minnow under a bobber is the great equalizer. There's no shame in it — guides do it.

### 3. Slow Down

The #1 beginner error is fishing too fast. A bass will watch a lure swim by six times and eat it on the seventh — but only if it's moving slow enough to catch. Cast, let it sit, twitch it, let it sit longer than feels reasonable. Count to ten between movements. Then count to twenty. Slow is almost always the answer.

### 4. Move Spots

Give a spot **20 to 30 minutes** of real effort. If nothing — not a bite, not a follow, not a swirl — move. Fish the shade line, the dock pilings, the fallen tree, the weed edge, the point where the wind is blowing into the bank (wind pushes baitfish, baitfish bring predators). Cover water until you find them, then slow down and work the area that produced.

### 5. Check the Conditions

* **Cold front just passed?** Fish get lockjaw for a day or two after a front. Fish tight to cover, go small, go slow.
* **Middle of a bright, calm midday?** Fish go deep or bury in shade. Fish the shadiest, deepest cover you can find — or take a lunch break and fish the evening bite instead.
* **Water suddenly muddy?** Switch to loud, bright, smelly: spinnerbaits, rattling crankbaits, stink bait for catfish.
* **Check your line and hooks.** A nicked line, a dull hook, or a lure fouled with weeds will cost you the one bite of the day. Retie and check often.

### 6. The Nuclear Option: Change Species

If the bass won't bite, the bluegill will. Some days the target species just isn't having it, and an hour of steady panfish action beats six hours of bass stubbornness — especially with kids. Rig up the bobber-and-worm, find the nearest dock, and have fun. The bass will be there tomorrow.

## Grab-and-Go Trip Checklists (printable)

Tear these out (or screenshot them) and run through them the night before and the morning of.

### The Night Before

- [ ] License valid and on your phone / in your wallet
- [ ] Checked size and bag limits for this water
- [ ] Weather forecast checked (morning and afternoon)
- [ ] Someone knows where you're going and when you'll be back
- [ ] Tackle audit: hooks sharp, rusted hooks replaced, lost lures restocked
- [ ] Line checked — last 2 feet smooth, or respooled
- [ ] Live bait bought or dug (nightcrawlers keep overnight in the fridge in damp newspaper)
- [ ] Cooler packed with ice, water, and snacks
- [ ] Batteries charged (phone, headlamp, fish finder if you use one)

### Morning Of — Load the Truck

- [ ] Rods (in cases) and reels
- [ ] Tackle box / bag
- [ ] Landing net
- [ ] Pliers, line clippers
- [ ] Life jackets — one per person
- [ ] First aid kit
- [ ] Sunscreen, hat, polarized sunglasses, bug spray
- [ ] Rain jacket (weather changes; be ready)
- [ ] Knife or multitool
- [ ] Tape measure
- [ ] Stringer / cooler for keepers
- [ ] Headlamp (even for day trips — you might stay for dusk)
- [ ] Phone charger or battery bank
- [ ] Trash bag (pack out everything, including line scraps — loose line kills wildlife)

### At the Water — Before the First Cast

- [ ] PFD on (boat/kayak) or accessible (bank)
- [ ] First knot tied and pull-tested
- [ ] Drag set — tight enough to set the hook, loose enough that a big fish can pull line (about 1/4 to 1/3 of your line's test strength)
- [ ] Bobber depth set / lure checked for weeds
- [ ] Phone in a zip bag or dry pouch
- [ ] Look around: any "no fishing" signs, private property lines, or hazards you missed?

*Camp like you've done it for 30 years — because now you can.*
