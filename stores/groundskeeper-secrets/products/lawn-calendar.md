## How to Use This Calendar

This calendar is written for **cool-season grasses** — tall fescue, Kentucky bluegrass, perennial ryegrass, and fine fescue — in the **Northeast US** (think New Jersey, Pennsylvania, New York, New England, and the Upper Midwest). If you're south of the transition zone growing bermuda or zoysia, this isn't your calendar.

Timing shifts about two weeks in either direction depending on your exact spot — a lawn near the coast wakes up earlier than one in the mountains. Watch your grass and your soil thermometer more than the calendar date. The months are guideposts, not gospel.

Each month gives you: the month's main job, mowing guidance, feeding, seeding, weed and pest notes, watering, and a quick checklist.

---

## The Big Rhythm of a Cool-Season Lawn

Cool-season grass does almost all its growing in two windows: **spring** (April–June) and **fall** (September–November). Summer is survival. Winter is sleep. Once you understand that rhythm, every decision gets easy:

- **Feed and seed in the growth windows** (spring lightly, fall heavily).
- **Protect in summer** (mow high, water deep, don't push growth).
- **Do nothing in winter** except stay off it.

Fall is the most important season of the year for your lawn. Spring is second. Everything in this calendar builds toward September.

## January — Plan

The lawn is asleep under frost or snow. Your job: think, not do.

**Main job:** Plan the year. Walk your property (from the driveway — stay off frozen grass, footprints on frozen turf break blades and leave dead marks) and note problem areas: thin spots, drainage puddles, heavy shade, grub damage from last summer.

**Mowing:** None. Mower stays parked.

**Feeding:** None.

**Seeding:** None — but this is a great month to order a [AFFILIATE: soil test kit] from your county extension office so it's ready for spring.

**Weeds & pests:** None.

**Watering:** None.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Walk the property and list problem areas
- [ ] Order a soil test kit
- [ ] Service the mower: change oil, replace spark plug and air filter, sharpen or replace the blade ([AFFILIATE: mower blade sharpener kit])
- [ ] Stay off frozen grass

## February — Prep

Still dormant. The itch to "do something" is strong — resist it.

**Main job:** Finish planning and buy early. Seed and fertilizer selection is best in late winter before the spring rush picks shelves clean.

**Mowing:** None.

**Feeding:** None.

**Seeding:** None. If you ordered seed, store it somewhere cool and dry — not a freezing garage or a hot shed.

**Weeds & pests:** As snow melts, watch for snow mold (matted, grayish patches). Light raking once things dry out helps; most lawns recover on their own.

**Watering:** None.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Buy seed and fertilizer now while selection is good
- [ ] Check mower service is done
- [ ] Lightly rake any matted snow-mold areas once dry
- [ ] Keep foot traffic off soft, thawing turf

## March — Wake Up

The lawn stirs. Clean-up month.

**Main job:** Spring cleanup. Rake up branches, debris, and matted leaves. Go easy — aggressive raking on wet, tender spring turf tears it up. A light pass is all you need.

**Mowing:** First mow when the grass starts actively growing. Cut at 2.5 to 3 inches — a bit lower than normal — to clean up winter stragglers and let sun hit the soil. Make sure that blade is sharp.

**Feeding:** Hold off unless your soil test says otherwise. If you put down a proper winterizer last November, the lawn is still eating it.

**Seeding:** Don't. Spring seeding fights crabgrass all season and the seedlings face summer with baby roots. (Exception: small emergency patches — but know it's an uphill battle.)

**Weeds & pests:** Pre-emergent crabgrass preventer goes down when soil temperatures hit about 55°F — traditionally when forsythia bushes bloom. That's your signal. Apply [AFFILIATE: pre-emergent crabgrass preventer] per the bag rate. Miss this window and crabgrass owns your summer.

**Watering:** Usually unnecessary — spring rain handles it.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Light spring cleanup rake
- [ ] First mow at 2.5–3" with a sharp blade
- [ ] Soil test results back? Follow them
- [ ] Pre-emergent down at forsythia bloom (~55°F soil)
- [ ] No spring seeding (unless patching emergencies)

## April — First Growth

Everything wakes up at once — grass, weeds, and your mower.

**Main job:** Mow, mow, mow. Spring flush growth means cutting every 5 to 7 days. Never cut more than one-third of the blade at once.

**Mowing:** 3 to 3.5 inches. This is the height sweet spot for spring — tall enough to shade out weed seeds, short enough to look sharp.

**Feeding:** Light feeding only if needed: 1/2 to 3/4 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft. If you winterized properly, you can skip this entirely — overfeeding spring grass creates soft, disease-prone growth.

**Seeding:** Still no (pre-emergent is down — it would block grass seed too).

**Weeds & pests:** Prime broadleaf weed season. Dandelions, clover, and chickweed are actively growing and vulnerable — spot-spray on a calm day when temps are 60–80°F. Don't blanket-spray the whole lawn if only a few weeds are up.

**Watering:** About 1 inch per week if rain doesn't provide it. Morning watering only.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Mow every 5–7 days at 3–3.5"
- [ ] Light fertilizer only if lawn looks hungry (1/2–3/4 lb N per 1,000 sq ft)
- [ ] Spot-treat broadleaf weeds in 60–80°F weather
- [ ] 1" of water per week if dry

## May — Pre-Emergent & Mowing Season

Late spring. The lawn should be looking its best right now — enjoy it.

**Main job:** Stay on the mowing schedule and keep the pre-emergent barrier intact.

**Mowing:** 3 to 3.5 inches, every 5–7 days. Leave the clippings — they're free fertilizer (about 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft over a season).

**Feeding:** None needed. Do NOT pile on fertilizer heading into summer — lush spring growth going into heat is a disease invitation.

**Seeding:** No.

**Weeds & pests:** If you split your pre-emergent into two applications, the second half goes down now (check the product label for split-application timing). Watch for dollar spot and other fungal issues as humidity climbs; proper mowing height and morning watering prevent most of it.

**Watering:** 1 inch per week if rain falls short. Set out a [AFFILIATE: rain gauge] and stop guessing.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Mow 3–3.5", leave clippings
- [ ] Second pre-emergent split app if that's your program
- [ ] 1" water per week if dry
- [ ] No heavy fertilizing

## June — Summer Prep

The pivot month. What you do now decides whether August is green or brown.

**Main job:** Raise the deck and armor against grubs.

**Mowing:** Raise to 3.5 to 4 inches for summer. Taller grass shades the soil, keeps roots cool, and holds moisture. This single change does more for summer survival than anything else.

**Feeding:** None. Fertilizing in summer heat pushes weak growth that disease loves.

**Seeding:** No.

**Weeds & pests:** Grub preventative window: mid-June through mid-July is the time for a preventative [AFFILIATE: grub control] application. Japanese beetles are laying eggs now; the grubs hatch in late summer and eat roots. One application now saves a destroyed lawn in September.

**Watering:** Deep and infrequent — 1 to 1.5 inches per week, in one or two morning sessions. Shallow daily sprinkling trains roots to stay at the surface where heat kills them.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Raise mowing height to 3.5–4"
- [ ] Grub preventative down (mid-June to mid-July)
- [ ] Deep watering: 1–1.5" per week, mornings
- [ ] No nitrogen fertilizer
- [ ] Keep that blade sharp
## July — Survival Mode

The toughest month. Your job is to keep the lawn alive, not to make it pretty.

**Main job:** Water wisely — or let it sleep.

**Mowing:** Stay at 3.5 to 4 inches. Mow less often if growth slows. Sharp blade is critical — dull blades shred heat-stressed grass.

**Feeding:** Absolutely none. Fertilizer + heat + humidity = brown patch and other fungal nightmares.

**Seeding:** No.

**Weeds & pests:** Hand-pull weeds; do NOT spray broadleaf herbicides in heat above 85°F — they'll scorch the good grass along with the weeds. Watch for brown patch (circular brown areas, often with a smoky ring in the morning) — the cure is less water at night and no nitrogen, not more chemicals.

**Watering:** Two legitimate strategies: (1) Water deeply — 1 to 1.5 inches per week in early morning — to stay green. (2) Let it go dormant and brown. Dormant grass isn't dead — but the crowns need about 1/2 inch of water every 2 to 3 weeks to stay alive. Pick one strategy and commit; flip-flopping stresses it most. Stay off dormant grass — foot traffic on brown turf kills crowns.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Mow high (3.5–4"), sharp blade, mow less often
- [ ] 1–1.5" water per week to stay green, OR 1/2" every 2–3 weeks to stay alive dormant
- [ ] No fertilizer, no weed spraying in heat
- [ ] Stay off dormant turf

## August — Get Ready for Fall

The comeback starts now. Everything this month points at September.

**Main job:** Prep for overseeding. Order your [AFFILIATE: tall fescue grass seed blend] now if you haven't. Get a soil test if you skipped spring. Line up a [AFFILIATE: core aerator rental] for early September; they sell out.

**Mowing:** Keep at 3.5" through the heat, then drop to 3" in late August as nights cool.

**Feeding:** None yet — wait for September.

**Seeding:** Not yet — but late August is go-time in the northernmost areas (see the Playbook's timing chapter).

**Weeds & pests:** Late August is the best time of year to kill perennial weeds like creeping Charlie and wild violet — they're pulling nutrients down to their roots for winter and take herbicide with it. Spot-treat on a cool evening. Grub damage shows up now (peeling turf like a carpet) — if you skipped the June preventative, a curative application can still help.

**Watering:** If the lawn went dormant, resume deep watering as temperatures cool to wake it up ahead of seeding. About 1 inch per week.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Order seed, book aerator rental
- [ ] Soil test if not done
- [ ] Spot-treat perennial weeds on cool evenings
- [ ] Resume watering to wake dormant turf
- [ ] Drop mowing height to 3" late in the month

## September — The Big Month

This is it — the most important month of the lawn year. Everything you've done (and not done) pays off here.

**Main job:** Aerate, overseed, fertilize, water. Follow *The Fall Overseeding Playbook* step by step — that's literally what it's for.

**Mowing:** Mow short (1.5–2") and bag clippings BEFORE overseeding. After seeding, wait until new grass hits 3 inches, then mow at 3" with a sharp blade.

**Feeding:** Starter fertilizer on seeding day — 1/2 to 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft. No weed-and-feed, ever, at seeding.

**Seeding:** Prime time. Soil 50–65°F, at least 45 days before first frost. Tall fescue 4–6 lbs, perennial rye 4–6 lbs, Kentucky bluegrass 1–2 lbs per 1,000 sq ft.

**Weeds & pests:** No pre-emergent (it blocks grass seed). No broadleaf spraying on new seedlings. Hand-pull only.

**Watering:** The critical job — light and frequent until germination (2–3x daily), then taper to 1 inch per week. See the Playbook's watering chapter.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Mow short + bag, dethatch if needed, core aerate
- [ ] Seed at proper rate, crosshatch pattern
- [ ] Starter fertilizer same day
- [ ] Water 2–3x daily until germination, then taper
- [ ] First mow when new grass hits 3"

## October — Leaves & New Grass

Protect the new seedlings and deal with the leaf avalanche.

**Main job:** Keep the new grass growing; manage leaves without smothering it.

**Mowing:** Back to 3–3.5" for established grass. For leaves: mulch-mow them — run the mower over fallen leaves and let the chopped bits filter into the turf. It's free organic matter and faster than raking. Only rake/bag when leaves are so thick the mower chokes.

**Feeding:** None this month — the winterizer comes in November.

**Seeding:** Early October spot-seeding of thin areas is fine IF you're still 45+ days from hard frost. After mid-October in most of the Northeast, put the seed away.

**Weeds & pests:** Hand-pull only around new seedlings. Established areas can get a broadleaf treatment on a calm, cool day if weeds are still actively growing.

**Watering:** Keep watering the new grass — 1 inch per week until growth stops. Don't assume fall rain is enough; check with the rain gauge.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Mulch-mow leaves (don't let them mat)
- [ ] Keep watering new seed: 1" per week
- [ ] Spot-seed early in the month only if window is open
- [ ] No fertilizer yet

---

## November — Winterize

Put the lawn to bed properly and it wakes up ahead of the neighbors.

**Main job:** Winterizer fertilizer + final mow + leaves OFF.

**Mowing:** Final cut at 2.5 inches — shorter than normal so grass doesn't mat under snow (matted grass = snow mold). Then clean and winterize the mower: stabilize the fuel or run it dry, per your manual.

**Feeding:** The winterizer — after top growth has stopped but grass is still green, apply about 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft. Highest-ROI feeding of the year.

**Seeding:** Done for the year.

**Weeds & pests:** Get the LAST leaves off. A leaf layer left all winter smothers grass and breeds disease. One final cleanup pass.

**Watering:** None needed — nature takes over. If you have in-ground irrigation, get it blown out before hard freezes.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Winterizer: ~1 lb N per 1,000 sq ft after growth stops
- [ ] Final mow at 2.5"
- [ ] All leaves removed
- [ ] Irrigation blown out; mower winterized

---

## December — Rest

Done. The lawn is asleep; you should be too (figuratively).

**Main job:** Nothing. Stay off frozen grass. Clean and store tools. Sharpen the mower blade now so spring-you says thanks.

**Mowing:** None.

**Feeding:** None.

**Seeding:** None.

**Weeds & pests:** None.

**Watering:** None.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Stay off frozen turf
- [ ] Tools cleaned, stored, blades sharpened
- [ ] Look back at the year: what worked? Note it for January planning
